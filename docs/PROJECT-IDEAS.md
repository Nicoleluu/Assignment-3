# Project ideas and rationale

## Central question

How can multiple computational structures change what a viewer notices about one ordinary design object?

The DCM is a useful subject because it is visually concise but historically and materially dense. Its two molded-plywood shells and thin steel frame can be read as drawing, volume, manufacturing logic, bodily support, a daily participant, a network of dependencies, and a product that moves through geographic and cultural systems.

## Seven lenses

### 1. Drawing

Reduce the chair to line, curve, proportion, lamination, and assembly. Interaction makes close looking active.

### 2. Volume

Return depth and viewer-controlled perspective. The three-dimensional model makes gaps, angles, and structural continuity inspectable.

### 3. Time

Describe the chair through a day of events rather than through appearance. Empty intervals matter as much as occupied ones.

### 4. Relationship

Make materials, processes, parts, comfort, and use mutually dependent. The force graph avoids a single fixed reading order.

### 5. Geography

Locate design, production, and collection as distinct contributors to the chair’s meaning and value.

### 6. Participation

Ask visitors to imagine the chair in their own lives. Aggregate answers create a small collective interpretation without collecting identity.

### 7. Conversation

Use an agent to redirect questions toward observation. The guide should support interpretation rather than replace it.

## Artistic and design references

- Charles and Ray Eames: material experimentation, iteration, and accessible industrial production.
- Mid-century furniture catalogues: restrained typography, orthographic imagery, and object-centered layouts.
- Contemporary digital exhibitions: scrolling as sequence, explicit interaction instructions, and layered documentation.
- Information design: small multiples, force-directed networks, timelines, and maps as different arguments rather than neutral containers.
